# 2.0 Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Mitigations

## 2.1 Compare and contrast common threat actors and motivations

**Threat actors:**
A **threat actor** (or malicious actor) is an entity responsible for an event that impacts the safety or security of another entity.

- **Nation-state:** Government-backed actors targeting national security interests.
- **Nation-state:** Government-sponsored actors.
  - **Attributes:** Highest sophistication, massive resources/funding, constant attacks.
  - **Focus:** National security, critical infrastructure (utilities, military), espionage, or war. Often classified as **Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs)**.
- **Unskilled attacker:** Low-skill individuals using pre-made tools or scripts.
- **Unskilled Attacker:** Often called "Script Kiddies."
  - **Attributes:** Low technical skill, uses pre-made tools/scripts, limited resources.
  - **Focus:** "The hunt," disruption, or data exfiltration. They look for "low-hanging fruit."
- **Hacktivist:** Attackers motivated by social or political ideology.
- **Hacktivist:** A hacker with a political or social purpose.
  - **Attributes:** Can be remarkably sophisticated, often external but can infiltrate as an insider.
  - **Focus:** Political agendas, social change, website defacement, or releasing private documents to cause embarrassment.
- **Insider threat:** Trusted users exploiting authorized access to harm.
- **Insider Threat:** A threat from within the organization (employee, contractor, partner).
  - **Attributes:** Institutional knowledge, legitimate access, medium sophistication.
  - **Focus:** Revenge (disgruntled employee) or financial gain. Can be malicious or unintentional (human error).
- **Organized crime:** Professional criminal groups focused on financial gain.
- **Organized Crime:** Professional criminals working for profit.
  - **Attributes:** Highly sophisticated, well-funded, structured hierarchy (specialized roles for hacking, selling data, support).
  - **Focus:** Financial gain, ransomware, identity theft, and fraud.
- **Shadow IT:** Unapproved technology used without IT department knowledge.
- **Shadow IT:** Unauthorized systems/apps used without IT department approval.
  - **Attributes:** Often non-malicious but bypasses security controls, creating vulnerabilities.
  - **Focus:** Innovation or convenience (e.g., using personal cloud storage for work files).

**Attributes of actors:**

- **Internal/external:** Originates from inside or outside the organization.
- **Internal/External:** Is the actor "inside the house" or outside trying to get in?
- **Resources/funding:** Financial and material backing available to the attacker.
- **Resources/Funding:** Ranges from no money (unskilled) to unlimited government budgets (nation-state).
- **Level of sophistication/capability:** The technical skill and complexity of the attacker.
- **Level of Sophistication/Capability:** Ability to write custom malware vs. blindly running scans.

**Motivations:**

- **Data exfiltration:** Unauthorized transfer of data out of a network.
- **Espionage:** Spying to obtain secret government or trade information.
- **Service disruption:** Intentionally interrupting the availability of systems or networks.
- **Blackmail:** Coercing victims by threatening to reveal compromising information.
- **Financial gain:** Attacking primarily to generate monetary profit.
- **Philosophical/political beliefs:** Actions driven by ideology or personal convictions.
- **Ethical:** Actions intended to help or improve security ("White Hat").
- **Revenge:** Retaliatory attacks seeking payback for perceived wrongs.
- **Disruption/chaos:** Causing confusion or disorder for its own sake.
- **War:** Cyber operations conducting military strategy or sabotage.

- **Data exfiltration:** Stealing data for sale or leverage.
- **Espionage:** Spying (common in nation-state/corporate rivalry).
- **Service disruption:** Causing chaos or protesting (DoS attacks).
- **Blackmail:** Threatening to release data unless paid (Ransomware/Extortion).
- **Financial gain:** Direct monetary theft or fraud.
- **Philosophical/Political/Ethical:** Acting on beliefs (Hacktivism/Whistleblowing).
- **Revenge:** Retaliation for a perceived wrong.

## 2.2 Explain common threat vectors and attack surfaces

A **Threat Vector** (or Attack Vector) is the method/path used to gain access or infect a target. The **Attack Surface** is the sum of all points where an unauthorized user can try to enter or extract data.

**Message-based:**

- **Email:** Attack delivery via electronic mail messages.
- **Short Message Service (SMS):** Attack delivery via mobile text messages.
- **Instant messaging (IM):** Attack delivery via real-time chat applications.
Message-based Vectors
- **Email:** Phishing links/attachments.
- **SMS (Smishing):** Links sent via text message.
- **Instant Messaging (IM):** Malware delivered through real-time chat.

**Image-based:** Malware embedded within digital image files.
**File-based:** Malware delivered via downloadable files or attachments.
**Voice call:** Attacks conducted over phone or VoIP systems.
**Removable device:** Malware introduced via USBs or external drives.
Image-based Vectors

- **SVG (Scalable Vector Graphics):** Images described in XML can contain HTML injection or malicious JavaScript.
- **Steganography:** Hiding malicious code within innocent-looking image files.
File-based Vectors
- **PDFs:** Can contain embedded malicious objects.
- **ZIP/RAR:** Compression hides malware from simple scans.
- **Office Documents:** Macros and add-ins can execute malicious code.
Voice Call (Vishing)
- **Vishing:** Phishing over the phone or voicemail (caller ID spoofing is common).
Removable Devices
- **USB Flash Drives:** Can infect air-gapped networks, act as "hacker keyboards" (Rubber Ducky), or exfiltrate data physically.

**Vulnerable software:**

- Client-based vs. agentless
- **Client-based:** Vulnerabilities within specific installed software agents.
- **Agentless:** Vulnerabilities exploited without installing local software.
Vulnerable Software
- **Client-based:** Vulnerabilities in installed executables requiring constant patching.
- **Agentless:** Compromised software on the server affects all users without local installs.

Unsupported systems and applications
**Unsupported systems:** Software no longer receiving manufacturer security updates.
Unsupported Systems (End-of-Life)

- Systems no longer receiving security patches (e.g., Windows XP). A single unpatched system can be an entry point.

**Unsecure networks**

- **Wireless:** Attacks exploiting Wi-Fi radio signals.
- **Wired:** Attacks requiring physical connection to cables.
- **Bluetooth:** Attacks exploiting short-range wireless device pairing.
Unsecure Networks
- **Wireless:** Outdated protocols (WEP, WPA) or rogue access points.
- **Wired:** Interfaces without 802.1X protection.
- **Bluetooth:** Implementation flaws allowing eavesdropping or unauthorized access.

**Open service ports:** Network endpoints accepting traffic without restriction.

- **Open Ports:** Every active TCP/UDP port is an opportunity (e.g., Port 80, 443).

**Default credentials:** Standard usernames/passwords left unchanged by administrators.

- **Default Credentials:** Many routers/IOT devices have "admin/admin" defaults that must be changed.

**Supply chain**

- **Managed service providers (MSPs):** Third-party companies managing IT services for clients.
- **Vendors:** Sellers providing finished hardware or software products.
- **Suppliers:** Entities providing raw materials or components.
- **MSPs:** Gaining access to multiple customer networks via a single provider breach.
- **Vendors/Suppliers:** Counterfeit hardware with backdoors or compromised software updates (e.g., SolarWinds attack).

**Human vectors/social engineering:**

- **Phishing:** Deceptive emails inducing users to reveal information.
- **Vishing:** Phishing attempts conducted via voice phone calls.
- **Smishing:** Phishing attempts conducted via SMS text messages.
- **Misinformation/disinformation:** Spreading false info to deceive or confuse.
- **Impersonation:** Pretending to be another person or entity.
- **Business email compromise (BEC):** Compromising legitimate email accounts to conduct fraud.
- **Pretexting:** Fabricating a scenario to gain victim's trust.
- **Watering hole:** Infecting websites frequently visited by targets.
- **Brand impersonation:** Mimicking a trusted company to trick users.
- **Typosquatting:** Registering domains visually similar to popular sites.
- **Phishing:** Spoofing and misdirection (check for spelling/URL errors).
- **Business Email Compromise (BEC):** Spoofing executives to authorize fraudulent wire transfers.
- **Pretexting:** Creating a character and a story to trap a victim (e.g., "I'm with the help desk").
- **Impersonation:** Pretending to be someone of higher rank or a "buddy."
- **Watering Hole:** Infecting a 3rd-party website that the target group frequently visits.
- **Typosquatting:** Registering domains similar to legitimate ones (URL Hijacking).
- **Brand Impersonation:** Mimicking famous brands to trick users into pop-ups/malware.

## 2.3 Explain various types of vulnerabilities

**Application:**

- **Memory injection:** Inserting malicious code into running application memory.
- **Buffer overflow:** Writing more data than a memory block holds.
- **Race conditions:** Outcome depends on uncontrollable event timing sequence.
  - **Time-of-check (TOC):** The moment the system validates a request.
  - **Time-of-use (TOU):** The moment the system acts on resources.
- **Malicious update:** Legitimate-looking software patch containing hidden malware.
- **Memory Injection:** Adding code into the memory of a running process to hide malware or escalate privileges (e.g., **DLL Injection**).
- **Buffer Overflow:** Overwriting a memory buffer to spill into other areas; developers must use **bounds checking** to prevent this.
- **Race Conditions:** Timing-based bugs where the outcome depends on the sequence of events.
  - **TOCTOU (Time-of-Check to Time-of-Use):** Something changes between the system checking a resource and actually using it.
- **Malicious Update:** Fake updates that weaken security; prevented by **code signing**.

**Operating system (OS)-based:** Flaws specific to the underlying system software.

- **OS-based:** Complex codebases with millions of lines often have hidden flaws (Remote Code Execution, Privilege Elevation).

**Web-based:** Flaws within web applications or browsers.

- **SQL injection (SQLi):** Injecting queries to manipulate databases via input.
- **Cross-site scripting (XSS):** Injecting malicious scripts into trusted websites.
  - **SQL Injection (SQLi):** Inserting SQL commands into input fields to manipulate the database (e.g., `' OR '1'='1'`).
  - **Cross-site Scripting (XSS):** Injecting scripts into trusted websites to steal cookies/sessions.
    - **Reflected (Non-persistent):** Script is in user input (e.g., a search box) and reflected back.
    - **Stored (Persistent):** Script is saved on the server (e.g., a forum post) and affects all visitors.

Hardware

- **Firmware:** Software permanently programmed into hardware read-only memory.
- **End-of-life:** Vendor stops supporting or updating the product.
- **Legacy:** Outdated technology still in use, often insecure.
- **Hardware:** Firmware vulnerabilities, End-of-life (EOL) gear, or legacy hardware that cannot support new security measures.

**Virtualization:** Creating virtual versions of physical computing resources.

- **Virtual machine (VM) escape:** Breaking out of VM to access host.
- **Resource reuse:** Data persisting after memory is supposedly freed.
- **VM Escape:** Breaking out of a VM to interact with the host system.
- **Resource Reuse:** Sensitive data remaining in memory/storage and accessed by other processes.

**Cloud-specific:** Vulnerabilities unique to shared cloud computing environments.

- **Cloud-specific:** Misconfigurations (public S3 buckets), insecure APIs, account hijacking.

**Supply chain:** Risks inherited from third-party vendors and products.

- **Supply Chain:** Risks from Service/Hardware/Software providers (e.g., backdoors in chips).

- **Service provider:** External entity delivering IT or business services.
- **Hardware provider:** Manufacturer supplying physical components or devices.
- **Software provider:** Developer supplying applications or code libraries.

**Cryptographic:** Flaws in encryption algorithms or implementation.

- **Weak Algorithms:** Using DES or MD5 (subject to collisions).
- **Poor Implementation:** Programming errors like buffer overflows in encryption code.
- **Key Management:** Improperly stored or generated keys.
**Misconfiguration:** Incorrectly set security settings leaving gaps.
- **Misconfiguration:** Using default settings or leaving unnecessary services enabled.

**Mobile device:** Risks specific to smartphones and tablets.

- **Side loading:** Installing apps from unofficial sources.
- **Jailbreaking:** Removing manufacturer restrictions on mobile devices.
- **Mobile Device:** Side-loading (untrusted apps), Jailbreaking/Rooting (removing OS restrictions).

**Zero-day:** Vulnerability unknown to the vendor or developer.
Zero-day

- A vulnerability unknown to the vendor with "zero days" to fix before exploitation begins.

## 2.4 Given a scenario, analyze indicators of malicious activity

**Malware attacks:**

- **Ransomware:** Malware demanding payment to restore data access.
- **Trojan:** Malware disguised as legitimate, harmless software.
- **Worm:** Self-replicating malware spreading independently across networks.
- **Spyware:** Malware secretly recording user activity and data.
- **Bloatware:** Unwanted, pre-installed software consuming system resources.
- **Virus:** Malicious code attaching to host files to replicate.
- **Keylogger:** Software recording keystrokes to steal credentials.
- **Logic bomb:** Malicious code triggered by specific conditions or time.
- **Rootkit:** Malware providing privileged access while hiding itself.
- **Ransomware:** Sudden file encryption, ransom notes, changed extensions.
- **Trojan:** Disguised as legitimate software; unauthorized system changes.
- **Worm:** Rapid independent spread across the network; high bandwidth usage.
- **Spyware:** Keyloggers, unauthorized surveillance (camera/mic), pop-ups.
- **Bloatware:** Unwanted pre-installed apps slowing down systems.
- **Virus:** Attaches to files; corrupts data or alters program behavior.
- **Logic Bomb:** Triggered by a specific date/time or event (e.g., employee termination).
- **Rootkit:** Clandestine access; hides itself from the OS and scanners.

**Physical attacks:**

- **Brute force:** Forcing entry through physical barriers or locks.
- **RFID cloning:** Copying data from radio frequency identification chips.
- **Environmental:** Manipulating temperature, power, or humidity to damage systems.

**Network attacks:**

- **Distributed denial-of-service (DDoS):** Overwhelming a target with traffic from many sources.
  - **Amplified:** Increasing traffic volume via response-heavy protocols.
  - **Reflected:** Spoofing victim IP to solicit floods of responses.
- **DNS attacks:** Manipulating domain resolution to redirect traffic.
- **Wireless:** Exploiting vulnerabilities in radio-based network communications.
- **On-path:** Intercepting and altering communications between two parties.
- **Credential replay:** Capturing and reusing valid login data.
- **Malicious code:** Harmful scripts or programs designed to damage.
- **DDoS:** Overwhelming traffic (SYN Floods, UDP Floods).
  - **Amplified/Reflected:** Using spoofed IPs to force 3rd-party servers to flood the victim.
- **DNS Attacks:** Cache Poisoning (Spoofing), DNS Hijacking, or DNS Tunneling (exfiltrating data in DNS queries).
- **On-path (MitM):** Eavesdropping, session hijacking, or **SSL Stripping** (downgrading HTTPS to HTTP).
- **Credential Replay:** Reusing captured credentials (prevented by MFA or salts).

**Application attacks:**

- **Injection:** Inserting malicious input to manipulate application execution.
- **Buffer overflow:** Overwriting memory bounds to crash or control execution.
- **Replay:** Resending captured data to impersonate legitimate user.
- **Privilege escalation:** Gaining higher access rights than originally authorized.
- **Forgery:** Falsifying data or signatures to deceive systems.
- **Directory traversal:** Accessing unauthorized files by climbing directory trees.
- **Privilege Escalation:**
  - **Vertical:** Regular user becomes Admin.
  - **Horizontal:** User accesses another user's data.
- **Forgery:** Cross-site Request Forgery (CSRF) or Server-side Request Forgery (SSRF).
- **Directory Traversal:** Using `../` sequences to access files outside the web root.

**Cryptographic attacks:**

- **Downgrade:** Forcing system to use weaker, older security protocols.
- **Collision:** Two distinct inputs producing the same hash value.
- **Birthday:** Exploiting probability to find hash collisions faster.

**Password attacks:**

- **Spraying:** Trying one common password against many accounts.
- **Brute force:** Trying all possible combinations to guess passwords.

**Indicators:**

- **Account lockout:** Access disabled after multiple failed login attempts.
- **Concurrent session usage:** Multiple simultaneous logins using the same credentials.
- **Blocked content:** Firewall or filter preventing access to specific data.
- **Impossible travel:** Logins from geographically distant locations too quickly.
- **Resource consumption:** Abnormal usage of CPU, memory, or bandwidth.
- **Resource inaccessibility:** Systems or data becoming unavailable to legitimate users.
- **Out-of-cycle logging:** Events recorded outside normal operational hours.
- **Published/documented:** Threat indicators listed in public security intelligence feeds.
- **Missing logs:** Gaps in records indicating potential tampering or deletion.
Indicators of Compromise (IoC)
- **Account Lockout:** Multiple failed login attempts.
- **Concurrent Session Usage:** One account logged in from multiple places.
- **Impossible Travel:** Logins from distant locations in a short timeframe.
- **Resource Consumption:** High CPU/Memory usage without cause.
- **Out-of-cycle Logging:** Logs generated at odd hours or deleted (Missing logs).

## 2.5 Explain the purpose of mitigation techniques used to secure the enterprise

**Segmentation:** Dividing networks to isolate traffic and limit spread.

- **Segmentation:** Dividing networks to isolate breaches (VLANs).

**Access control:** Regulating who or what can view resources.

- **Access Control:** Using **ACLs** and **Permissions** to regulate access.

- **Access control list (ACL):** Table of rules defining rights for users.
- **Permissions:** Specific actions a user is allowed to perform.

**Application allow list:** Only pre-approved software can run on systems.  

- **Application Allow List:** Strict control where only approved apps run.
**Isolation:** Separating systems entirely to contain potential compromises.
- **Isolation/Sandboxing:** Running untrusted code in a "safe space."
**Patching:** Applying updates to fix security vulnerabilities.
**Encryption:** Encoding data so only authorized parties can read.
**Monitoring:** Continuous observation of systems for suspicious activity.
**Least privilege:** Granting only minimum necessary access rights.
**Configuration enforcement:** Automating settings to ensure compliance with standards.
**Decommissioning:** Securely removing and destroying old systems or data.

Hardening techniques
**Hardening:** Reducing attack surface by securing system configurations.

- **Encryption (Host):** Protecting local data at rest on the device.
- **Installation of endpoint protection:** Deploying antivirus/antimalware directly on host devices.
- **Endpoint Protection (EPP/EDR):** Real-time threat detection and removal on the device.
- **Host-based firewall:** Software controlling traffic entering/leaving a single device.
- **Host-based Firewall/HIPS:** Machine-level traffic and activity control.
- **Host-based intrusion prevention system (HIPS):** Detects and blocks malicious activity on a host.
- **Disabling ports/protocols:** Turning off unused network services to reduce risk.
- **Disabling Ports/Protocols:** Closing unnecessary entry points.
- **Default password changes:** Replacing vendor-set credentials with secure, unique ones.
- **Default Password Changes:** Essential first step for any new device.
- **Removal of unnecessary software:** Uninstalling unused apps to eliminate potential vulnerabilities.
- **Hardening:** Reducing the attack surface by securing configurations.
- **Patching:** Regularly updating to fix vulnerabilities.
- **Encryption:** Protecting data-at-rest and data-in-transit.
- **Monitoring:** SIEM, Log Aggregation, and alert response.
- **Least Privilege:** Granting minimum necessary access.
- **Configuration Enforcement:** Standardizing settings via Group Policy or automation.
- **Decommissioning:** Securely wiping/destroying assets (Sanitization).
